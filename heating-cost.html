Read the results with these caveats. Firewood is often sold by the stère, not by weight — convert first (a stère of stacked seasoned hardwood is very roughly 350–500 kg, depending on species and stacking). Moisture matters enormously: wet wood can lose a third of its usable energy to evaporating its own water. The efficiency figures are typical values for each appliance type, not measurements of your specific one. And a heat pump's SCOP depends heavily on the emitter system — underfloor heating gets the high numbers, small old radiators do not.

Why the invoice price is the wrong comparison

Suppose heating oil costs €1.10 a litre and wood pellets cost €0.40 a kilogram. Pellets look like a third of the price — but a litre and a kilogram aren't the same amount of anything, least of all heat. A litre of heating oil holds about 10 kWh of energy; a kilogram of pellets holds about 4.8. Burn each in its usual appliance — a condensing oil boiler at around 90% efficiency, a pellet boiler at around 85% — and the oil delivers heat at roughly 12.2 cents per kWh while the pellets deliver at roughly 9.8. Still cheaper, but by a fifth, not by two-thirds. Every pairwise fuel comparison hides the same two conversions, and skipping them doesn't simplify the question — it answers a different one.

What a litre, a kilo and a cubic metre actually hold

Energy content is just a property of the fuel. A cubic metre of natural gas holds about 11 kWh (which is why gas bills in most of Europe are already converted to kWh for you — if yours is, use the "billed in kWh" row). A litre of heating oil holds about 10 kWh, a kilogram of LPG about 12.8, a kilogram of wood pellets about 4.8, and a kilogram of seasoned firewood at 20% moisture about 4. Electricity arrives already measured in kWh. Once everything is in kWh of fuel energy, half the conversion is done.

Efficiency: where the missing heat goes

The other half is the appliance, because no boiler turns all of the fuel's energy into heat in your radiators. Most of the loss goes straight up the flue as hot exhaust gas. A modern condensing boiler claws some of it back by cooling the exhaust until its water vapour condenses, reaching about 90%; an older non-condensing boiler manages about 75%. A good pellet boiler sits around 85%, a modern wood stove around 70%. An open fireplace is the extreme case: about 15% of the wood's energy heats the room, and the chimney's draught pulls already-warmed room air out of the house along with the smoke. That's why an open fire at a cheap wood price can land above electric resistance heating in the table — a result that surprises almost everyone the first time they see it.

Heat pumps: efficiency above 1 is not a trick

A heat pump's line in the table uses an "efficiency" of 3.5 or 4.5 — more heat out than electricity in. That isn't creative accounting. A heat pump doesn't create heat from electricity; it uses electricity to move heat that already exists outside — in the air or the ground — into the house, the way a fridge moves heat out of its own interior. Moving three or four units of heat costs about one unit of electricity, and the seasonal average of that ratio is the SCOP. The same arithmetic that handles a boiler handles a heat pump unchanged, which is exactly why cost-per-kWh-of-delivered-heat is the right scale: it makes fuels that burn and machines that pump comparable on one line. The honest caveat is that SCOP depends on the temperature the emitters need. Underfloor heating running at 35 °C gets the high numbers; small, old radiators that need 60 °C water can drag an air-source unit's real-world figure well below its brochure.

The firewood unit problem

Firewood prices are quoted in units that sound interchangeable and aren't. A stère is one cubic metre of stacked wood cut to one-metre lengths — restack the same wood cut shorter and it packs tighter, so the same stère of wood occupies less space. A "loose cubic metre" (wood tipped in a heap) contains perhaps 60–70% of the wood in a stacked one. So a price per "cubic metre" is meaningless until you know which cubic metre, and comparing two firewood offers by volume price is guesswork. Weight, at a known moisture content, is the only firm basis — which is why this tool prices firewood per kilogram and why the moisture caveat above is not optional fine print. Freshly felled wood is roughly half water, and every kilogram of that water steals about 0.7 kWh from the fire to boil it off before the wood can heat the room.

What this calculation deliberately ignores

This tool compares running cost per kWh of heat, and nothing else. It ignores the purchase and installation cost of the appliance — a ground-source heat pump's excellent running cost comes after a five-figure installation that a gas boiler doesn't need. It ignores maintenance, chimney sweeping, and the standing charges on gas and electricity contracts, which you pay even in July. And it ignores the unpriced labour of solid fuel: stacking wood, feeding a stove, clearing ash. A complete cost of ownership includes all of that; this table is the fuel-cost line of it, done properly. Saying so plainly is the difference between an honest tool and a sales page for whichever appliance the calculator's owner happens to sell.
