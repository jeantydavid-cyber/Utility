What mayonnaise actually is, and why it breaks

Mayonnaise is an emulsion: countless microscopic droplets of oil suspended in a small amount of water — the water in the yolk, the mustard and the lemon juice or vinegar. That's worth pausing on, because the proportions make it sound backwards. A finished mayonnaise is roughly three-quarters oil, yet the oil is the suspended phase and the water is the continuous one. The thickness of mayonnaise is exactly this crowding: so many oil droplets packed into so little water that they jam against each other and the mixture stops flowing like a liquid.

Oil and water famously don't mix, so something has to hold the droplets apart. That something is mostly lecithin, an emulsifier from the egg yolk. One end of a lecithin molecule is attracted to oil, the other to water, so it settles on the surface of each oil droplet and forms a coat that stops droplets merging back together when they touch. The yolk supplies a finite amount of this coating material, which is why the calculator above works from the oil quantity: each yolk can coat the droplets of about 200 ml of oil comfortably. Push far past that and the coats get thin and the emulsion fragile.

Breaking happens when oil arrives faster than it can be broken up and coated. At the start there is very little water phase, so the first oil must go in drop by drop while the whisk shears it into droplets. Add a glug too early and the balance tips: the oil pools instead of dispersing, the emulsion inverts or never forms, and you get the greasy, curdled liquid every cook eventually meets. The mustard isn't just for flavour — its mucilage is itself a mild emulsifier and stabiliser, which is why a mayonnaise started on a spoonful of mustard is noticeably harder to break. Temperature matters too: a fridge-cold yolk is stiffer, its lecithin disperses sluggishly, and cold oil is more viscous and harder to shear into fine droplets. Room-temperature ingredients are cheap insurance.

Why the rescue works

The standard rescue — a fresh yolk or a spoonful of water in a clean bowl, with the broken batch whisked in gradually — looks like magic and is actually bookkeeping. You are not repairing the broken emulsion. You are starting a brand-new one, and using the broken batch as its oil supply. The failed mayonnaise is, after all, mostly oil in a disorganised state; drizzled slowly into a fresh water phase with fresh emulsifier, it disperses and gets coated exactly as plain oil would. Once you see the rescue that way, you also see why it fails when rushed: pour the broken batch in quickly and you've simply repeated the original mistake into a second bowl.

The same principle next door

Most kitchen "curdling" disasters are the same physics wearing different costumes, and the same logic rescues them. Hollandaise splits when the butterfat separates from its water phase — usually from heat rather than haste — and is restarted the same way: a teaspoon of warm water in a clean bowl, the split sauce whisked in slowly. A split ganache, where the chocolate's fat has broken out of the cream, comes back when whisked gradually into a little warm cream or milk — fresh water phase, same trick. Seized chocolate looks like the opposite problem, a tiny splash of water turning silky chocolate into cement, but the counterintuitive fix is more water: enough to dissolve the sugar syrup clumps and carry everything as a smooth sauce again. And a curdled buttercream — cold butter beaten into meringue, breaking into greasy flecks — usually needs nothing but patience and a gentle warming of the bowl until the butter is soft enough to emulsify. In every case the question is the same one mayonnaise teaches: which phase is meant to be continuous, and does it have enough water and emulsifier to hold the fat? Fix that, and the sauce follows.
