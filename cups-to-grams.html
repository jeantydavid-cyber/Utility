Cups to grams, by ingredient

A cup is a volume, so converting it to weight needs the density of the specific ingredient. Converters that apply one number to everything are using water's density — wrong for nearly everything, and worst in baking, where precision matters most.

Why one conversion factor can't work

Take a US cup — 240 millilitres — and fill it twice: once with honey, once with cocoa powder. The honey weighs about 340 grams. The cocoa weighs about 85. Same cup, same volume, nearly four times the weight. Any converter that gives you one answer for "1 cup in grams" has silently picked a density, and almost always it has picked water's: 240 grams per cup. That's roughly correct for milk, close enough for yoghurt, badly wrong for flour, and catastrophic for anything fluffy or syrupy.

This is why volume-to-weight conversion has to be done per ingredient. Weight-to-weight conversion doesn't have this problem — a gram of feathers and a gram of lead famously weigh the same — but American recipes measure almost everything by volume, so cooks everywhere else are stuck doing this conversion, and doing it with the right density is the whole game.

The flour packing problem

Flour is the worst case, and not only because it's the ingredient where accuracy matters most. Flour compresses. A cup filled by spooning flour in loosely and levelling the top holds about 120 grams of all-purpose flour. A cup filled by dragging it through the bag — which is what most people naturally do — packs the flour down and can hold 140 grams or more. That's a 15–20% overdose of the ingredient that provides a baked good's structure, and it's why so many home-baked cakes come out dense and dry from a recipe that works fine in the author's kitchen. The numbers in this tool assume the spooned-and-levelled method, because that's what almost all American recipe developers use. Weighing the flour makes the entire problem disappear, permanently: 120 grams is 120 grams however you got it into the bowl.

Which cup? There are three

The cup itself isn't one thing. The US customary cup is 236.6 ml, rounded to 240 ml on nutrition labels and in practice. The metric cup, standard in Australia, New Zealand and much modern Commonwealth cooking, is 250 ml — about 4% larger. And old British cookbooks, printed before decimalisation, use the imperial cup of 284 ml (half an imperial pint), which is a full 18% larger than the US cup. A pre-1970s British recipe converted with US assumptions will be noticeably off in every ingredient at once. This tool handles the first two directly; for an imperial-cup recipe, multiply the result by 1.18.

Ounces versus fluid ounces

English also managed to give the same name to a unit of weight and a unit of volume. An ounce (weight) is 28.35 grams of anything. A fluid ounce is 29.57 millilitres of space. For water they nearly coincide, which is exactly why the confusion survives — and why it causes real disasters with anything that isn't water. When a recipe says "8 oz milk" it almost certainly means fluid ounces (a US cup); "8 oz cheese" means weight; "8 oz honey" is genuinely ambiguous, and the two readings differ by half again: 8 fl oz of honey weighs about 335 g, while 8 oz by weight is 227 g. In this tool, "ounces (weight)" converts directly to grams and ignores the ingredient entirely, while "fluid ounces" is a volume and goes through the ingredient's density like cups do. If a result looks strange, check you've picked the right ounce.

The tablespoon isn't safe either

A tablespoon is 15 ml in the US, the UK and Europe — but 20 ml in Australia. That's a 33% difference in a measure that recipes use for exactly the ingredients where 33% matters: baking powder, bicarbonate of soda, salt, yeast. An Australian recipe's tablespoon of baking powder, measured with a European spoon, under-raises the cake; the reverse direction over-raises it into a collapse. This tool uses the 15 ml convention (1/16 of a US cup). For an Australian recipe, add a third.

The honest advice

This converter exists because volume measurement of dry goods is a bad system that a lot of good recipes are written in. It is a workaround, not a solution. The solution costs about €15 and sits flat in a drawer: a digital kitchen scale. Weigh into the bowl on the scale, zero it between ingredients, and you'll never wash a measuring cup or wonder about packing, cup standards or spoon sizes again. Until the recipe you love is rewritten in grams, though — this page will do the arithmetic with the right density.
